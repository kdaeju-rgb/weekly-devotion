Small Group Bible Study

Not Excuses, but Confession

Sunday Worship · November 1, 2026
Text Acts 24:1–15 (ESV) · See also Acts 24:16, 21, 25; 1 Peter 3:16; Romans 1:16; Galatians 2:20
1Opening Up

Have you ever heard about someone before you actually met them? Share a time when the person turned out to be completely different from what you had heard, or a time when you were misunderstood because of something you never said.

2Reading the Passage

Take turns reading Acts 24:1–16 aloud, a few verses each. Then read verses 14–16 together once more, and notice how Paul’s words differ from Tertullus’s.

  • 1–4The lawyer Tertullus flatters Governor Felix
  • 5–9False labels on Paul: “a plague,” “a ringleader of the sect”
  • 10–13Paul states only the facts, with no flattery and no counterattack
  • 14–16“But this I confess to you”: the Way, the hope of resurrection, a clear conscience
3Message Summary

The hope of resurrection turns excuses into confession

At Paul’s trial before the Roman governor Felix, the lawyer Tertullus opens with flattery everyone knows is untrue, then labels Paul without a shred of evidence: “a plague, one who stirs up riots, a ringleader of the sect.” Then as now, the world tears people down by labeling them first and letting the crowd do the judging. But Paul does not pick up the same weapons. He neither flatters nor strikes back; he calmly states the facts. A Christian answers slander not with a louder voice but with a more honest life. Then Paul goes one step further: “But this I confess to you.” An excuse is a word that protects me; a confession is a word that reveals whose I am. Our lives are often a cause for shame, but the gospel we follow never is.

The real reason Paul stood trial was “the resurrection of the dead.” To believe in the resurrection is to believe that the final account of my life will be settled by God, not by people. That is why Paul says, “So I always take pains to have a clear conscience toward both God and man.” The word means to train, as an athlete trains the body every day. Ignore the pain long enough, and the sharp corners of the triangle of conscience wear down until it no longer pricks, like Felix, who heard the gospel for two years yet kept saying, “When I get an opportunity I will summon you,” and missed his chance. As Polycarp confessed, “How can I blaspheme my King who saved me?” Courage comes not from a loud voice but from a conscience with nothing to hide, and that conscience comes from the hope of resurrection.

“So I always take pains to have a clear conscience toward both God and man.”
Acts 24:16
4Observation Questions
  1. How does Tertullus open the trial, and what labels does he attach to Paul? What is missing from his accusation? Acts 24:2–9
  2. How is Paul’s reply different from Tertullus’s speech? Starting with “But this I confess to you” in verse 14, what changes in what Paul says? Acts 24:10–15
  3. In verse 16, what does “so” point back to? How is the hope of resurrection connected to a clear conscience “toward both God and man”? Acts 24:15–16, 21
5Life Application
  1. “Well, that’s what everyone says.” Have you ever passed along something you hadn’t verified, or picked up the same weapons when you were wronged? What would change if you responded the way Paul did?
  2. Have you ever downplayed your faith, almost making excuses for it, at work, at school, or with family who don’t believe?
    What would it look like to admit your own shortcomings while never being ashamed of the gospel?
  3. Is there something that once troubled your conscience but no longer bothers you, a corner that has worn smooth? Like Felix, is there an act of obedience you keep putting off “until I get an opportunity”? Take one step this week.
6Let’s Pray Together
  • For those worn down by misunderstanding and unfair treatment, that they would not collapse but trust that people’s judgment is not the final word
  • That we would not repeat unverified words, and would answer injustice not with a louder voice but with a more honest life
  • That we would not hide our life of following Jesus, but confess the gospel with gentleness, never rudeness
  • That the hope of resurrection would move us to train daily for a clear conscience before God and people
7Closing Prayer

Father God, thank You for placing us before Your Word, we who have so often given our hearts to the approval and opinions of others. Have mercy on us, who have let a single remark decide our worth, and who have been gripped by anger and fear whenever we were misunderstood. When we are wronged, give us wisdom to speak the truth honestly, and keep us from insulting others or twisting the truth to protect ourselves. If we have hurt someone by repeating words we never checked, help us to see it and turn back. When we are rightly corrected, let repentance come before excuses. Keep us from hiding our life of following You to win people’s favor, and help us bear witness to You with honest and gentle words and deeds. We put our hope in You, the God who raises the dead. Remembering that we too will stand before You, give us grace to keep our promises, honor the labor of others, and set right our wrongs at home, at work, at school, and in the church. Make our church not a place that hides the truth to protect its reputation, but a place where people are restored in truth and grace. We pray in the name of Jesus Christ, who saved us on the cross and is our living hope of resurrection.

Amen.
Leader’s Notes

The Surrounding Context (23:23–24:27)

In last week’s passage (23:11) the Lord promised, “so you must testify also in Rome.” Escaping a plot by more than forty men, Paul was sent under armed escort to Governor Felix in Caesarea (23:23–35). Five days later the high priest Ananias came down with elders and a lawyer named Tertullus (24:1). After the hearing Felix postponed his decision (24:22) and, with his wife Drusilla, heard Paul speak about faith in Christ (24:24–25). Yet he often sent for Paul only in hope of a bribe, and when Porcius Festus succeeded him two years later, Felix left Paul in prison to please the Jews (24:26–27).

Governor Felix

A former slave, Felix became governor of Judea (c. AD 52–59) thanks to his brother Pallas, who had great influence at the imperial court. The Roman historian Tacitus wrote that he “exercised the power of a king with the mind of a slave.” Riots and brutal crackdowns marked his rule, so Tertullus’s “through you we enjoy much peace” (v. 2) was flattery everyone recognized.

“The Way” and the Resurrection Dispute (vv. 14, 21)

The early believers called themselves followers of “the Way” (9:2; 19:9; 22:4), echoing Jesus’ words, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). Verse 21 refers to Paul’s cry before the council, “It is with respect to the hope and the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial” (23:6). Because the accusation was driven by the Sadducees, who deny the resurrection (23:8), the heart of the case was the risen Jesus.

“Take Pains” (askeō) and Polycarp

The Greek verb askeō appears only here in the New Testament and was used of an athlete’s training (it is the root of “ascetic”). Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, was martyred around AD 155. According to The Martyrdom of Polycarp, crowds called Christians “atheists” because they refused to worship the Roman gods. His full reply: “Eighty-six years I have served Him, and He has done me no wrong. How then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?”

Facilitation Tips

  • Suggested time: Opening 10 min · Reading & Summary 15 min · Observation 15 min · Application 20 min · Prayer 15 min
  • Keep the sermon’s balance in view. It does not say that every criticism of the church or of us is persecution. When our wrongs are exposed, we admit them and repent first; what should shame us is our hypocrisy, not the gospel.
  • Someone in the group may be facing misunderstanding right now. In Application 1, keep the focus on “How did I respond?” so the conversation does not turn into talk about a particular person.
  • Application 3 is an invitation, not a condemnation. Note that honesty is not a guarantee of success (Paul was still in chains), and encourage each person to share one small step.
