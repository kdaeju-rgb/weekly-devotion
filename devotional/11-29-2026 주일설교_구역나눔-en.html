Small Group Bible Study

A Christ Too Strange for Me

First Sunday of Advent · November 29, 2026
Text Acts 28:23–31 (ESV) · See also Romans 1:16
1Opening Up

From coffee orders to YouTube and Netflix recommendations to music playlists, what is something you’ve customized “just the way you like it” that feels most comfortable to you? On the other hand, is there something that felt strange or uncomfortable at first but has become precious to you now?

2Reading the Passage

Take turns reading Acts 28:16–31 aloud, a few verses each. Then read verses 23–24 and 31 together once more, and notice what Paul proclaimed and how people responded.

  • 23Many come on the appointed day; from morning till evening Paul testifies to the kingdom of God and tries to convince them about Jesus from the Law of Moses and the Prophets
  • 24–25“Some were convinced by what he said, but others disbelieved”; as they disagree and depart, Paul makes one final statement
  • 26–28Isaiah’s words: “You will indeed hear but never understand, and you will indeed see but never perceive”; God’s salvation sent to the Gentiles
  • 30–31Two whole years living at his own expense, welcoming all who came, teaching about the kingdom and the Lord Jesus Christ “with all boldness and without hindrance”
3Message Summary

Not a customized Jesus, but Jesus as He truly is

After eleven months of walking through Acts together, we come to its final chapter. Paul had appealed to Caesar, yet Acts never records a single line of his trial. The author’s concern was not a verdict of innocence in a worldly court, but to reveal at the very heart of the empire that the true sovereign is not the emperor but the risen Jesus Christ. From morning till evening Paul proclaimed two things: the kingdom of God has come, and Jesus, the King of that kingdom, has come. Yet the response was, “Some were convinced… but others disbelieved.” They heard but did not understand; they saw but did not perceive. Why? We live in a customized culture where we choose everything to suit our taste. So we put “I love you” into our shopping cart, but leave “Take up your cross” on the shelf. We create a middle-class Jesus who only adds blessing upon blessing, a success-driven Jesus who measures the church by numbers, and a therapeutic Jesus who only praises us, and we call it faith. But a customized Jesus is not the Messiah; he is merely a doll we have made.

The real Jesus overturned the tables in the temple, demanded absolute loyalty, and calls us to tear down and rebuild our values and priorities. Paul added no seasoning or sweetener to this gospel. He was not discouraged when people failed to recognize Jesus, and he was not ashamed of the gospel. So Acts ends with Paul, a prisoner, teaching “with all boldness and without hindrance.” In 2007, Joshua Bell, one of the world’s finest violinists, played for 43 minutes in a subway station. More than a thousand people passed by, but only seven stopped to listen. Jesus, too, comes into our ordinary lives in humble clothes. Eyes and ears that only look for a Jesus who fits our expectations and tastes will never recognize the real Jesus of the cross. To receive Jesus is to open our whole lives to Him—to stop living as we always have and to live by the principles of a new kingdom under the reign of a new King.

“…proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance.”
Acts 28:31
4Observation Questions
  1. From morning till evening, what did Paul testify to, and from what did he try to convince them about Jesus? What does it mean that these two things are the heart of the gospel? Acts 28:23 · Luke 2:1
  2. How did those who heard Paul respond?
    According to the words from Isaiah that Paul quoted, why do people hear without understanding? Acts 28:24–27
  3. Rather than the outcome of Paul’s trial, with what scene does Acts end?
    Why could Paul, though a prisoner, teach “with all boldness and without hindrance”? Acts 28:30–31 · Romans 1:16
5Life Application
  1. We gladly receive “I love you,” yet sometimes leave words like “Take up your cross” or “Sell your possessions” out of our shopping cart. Which of God’s words have you been quietly leaving out lately?
  2. Of the “middle-class Jesus,” the “success-driven Jesus,” and the “therapeutic Jesus,” which most resembles your own faith? In what ways does the real Jesus—who overturns tables and demands absolute loyalty—feel strange to you?
  3. Just as a thousand people walked past Joshua Bell, the Lord may come into our everyday lives in humble form. As Advent begins, what do you want to pause, and what do you want to listen for, so that you can recognize Him at home and at work this week?
6Praying Together
  • That we would repent of shaping Jesus to our own tastes, and open our hearts to the Word with nothing added
  • That God would remove our hardened hearts—hearts that do not weep when we should weep or dance when we should dance—and give us tender hearts
  • That our church, though it may look plain and foolish to the world, would not be ashamed of the gospel and would boldly proclaim the kingdom of God and Jesus our King
  • That during Advent we would recognize the Lord who comes into our everyday lives, and that the “Acts chapter 29” written by the Holy Spirit would continue in our group and our homes
7Closing Prayer

Heavenly Father, thank You for the grace of walking together through the long journey of Acts until today. Like Paul, who boldly proclaimed the kingdom of God in the storm, in prison, and in the very heart of the empire, we too have gathered today before the Lord who came as King. On this first Sunday of Advent, we confess that not Caesar, but Jesus Christ alone is the true King. Help us lay down our familiar ways of living and follow the principles of a new kingdom and the reign of a new King. We have gladly received the words “I love you,” yet pretended not to hear “Take up your cross.” We have shaped Jesus to our own tastes and called it faith. So our hearts grew hard—hearing without understanding, seeing without perceiving—and we did not weep when we should have wept, nor dance when we should have danced. Take away our hardened hearts and transform them into tender hearts. Though our church may look plain and foolish in the eyes of the world, make us a church that is not ashamed of the gospel. Help us trust the gospel with nothing added, the Word that is itself the power of God, and like Paul, proclaim the kingdom of God boldly and without hindrance to the very end. Acts chapter 28 has come to a close, but give us grace so that the Acts chapter 29 written by the Holy Spirit may continue in the lives of all Your people. We pray in the name of Jesus Christ, our true King.

Amen.
Leader’s Notes

Paul in Rome (28:16, 30)

When Paul arrived in Rome, he was allowed to stay by himself with the soldier who guarded him (v. 16), and for two whole years he lived there at his own expense and welcomed all who came to him (v. 30). Though under house arrest, he was free to receive visitors, so his home became his place of ministry. Many scholars believe Paul wrote the so-called “Prison Epistles”—Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and Philemon—during this period.

The Quotation from Isaiah 6:9–10 (28:25–27)

Paul quotes the words Isaiah heard when he was called, the same words Jesus quoted when explaining the parable of the sower (Matt. 13:14–15). The problem is not that people cannot physically hear, but that their hearts have grown dull, so they hear without understanding. This connects well with the sermon’s message that we fail to recognize a Jesus who does not fit our tastes. Note that verse 29 does not appear in the earliest manuscripts, so most English translations, including the ESV, omit it or place it in a footnote.

Caesar Augustus and Jesus (Luke 2:1)

Emperor Augustus was called “son of god” in Rome, and his birth and reign were proclaimed as “good news” (gospel) that brought peace to the world. When Luke deliberately dates Jesus’ birth to the time of “Caesar Augustus,” he sets the empire’s gospel against the gospel of the kingdom, and the king of the world against the true King. Acts ending in Rome completes this contrast like a pair of bookends.

“Without Hindrance”—the Last Word of Acts (28:31)

In the original Greek, the very last word of Acts is an adverb meaning “unhindered” (akōlytōs). The apostle is in chains, but the gospel is not bound (cf. 2 Tim. 2:9). Because Acts records neither the trial’s outcome nor Paul’s death, its open ending invites the story to continue as “Acts chapter 29,” written by the Holy Spirit through the church—the conclusion of the sermon.

Advent and Bonhoeffer

Advent means “coming.” During the four Sundays before Christmas, we remember the Lord who came to this earth and wait for the Lord who will come again. Today is the first Sunday. Bonhoeffer’s diagnosis of “cheap grace,” quoted in the sermon, comes from his book The Cost of Discipleship (1937), written as a warning to a German church that was accommodating the Nazi regime.

Facilitation Tips

  • Suggested timing: Opening 10 min · Reading & Summary 15 min · Observation 15 min · Application 20 min · Prayer 15 min
  • Since this is the last session in Acts after nearly a year, you might invite one or two people to briefly share their most memorable scene from Acts during the opening.
  • Application question 2 is not a time to criticize other churches or other people. Just as the preacher confessed his own temptation to “tame” Jesus, please share honestly first, creating an atmosphere of turning back together rather than judging.
  • Briefly retell the Joshua Bell story and ask, “Would I have stopped in that subway station?” It leads naturally into application question 3.
  • As you close, invite members to share at the next meeting any experience this week of meeting the Lord who came to them in an unexpected form.
