Small Group Bible Study

Pressing On Toward the Goal

Sunday Worship · November 8, 2026
Text Acts 25:6–12 (ESV) · See also Acts 25:3, 25–27; 23:11; Philippians 3:13–14
1Opening Up

Have you ever been so “crazy” about something that you lost track of time? A hobby, a subject, your work, a person: anything counts. Share what changed in you during that season.

2Reading the Passage

Take turns reading Acts 25:1–12 aloud, a few verses each. Then read verses 10–12 together once more, and consider who seems more free: Paul, the prisoner, or Festus, the judge.

  • 1–5The new governor Festus visits Jerusalem; a plot to ambush and kill Paul
  • 6–8Charges without proof, and Paul’s defense: “I have committed no offense”
  • 9–11Festus seeks to please the Jews; “I appeal to Caesar”
  • 12“To Caesar you have appealed; to Caesar you shall go”
3Message Summary

Our occupations may change, but the calling the Lord gives us never stops

There is a Korean saying: “If you are not crazy about it, you cannot reach it.” Because Paul was crazy about the gospel of Jesus Christ, even as a Roman prisoner he was used as an evangelist whose influence reached the whole world. If we have followed Jesus for years yet prayer, Scripture, and worship still feel awkward, the problem may not be a lack of ability but a lack of love. So what we need first is not more knowledge, but to admit that our hearts do not beat with love for the Lord, and to begin a true relationship of love with Him. Service and church life built without this foundation are a house built on sand. Our lives have both an occupation and a calling. Occupations can change many times, but a calling is not a task; it is a value: to be made complete as God’s people and to become like Jesus Christ. Paul’s occupation changed from unsentenced prisoner in Caesarea to a prisoner under house arrest in Rome, but his calling as an apostle who lived out and displayed the gospel never changed.

When the case was reopened after two years, the accusers still brought charges “that they could not prove,” because there had never been any proof. When Festus, wanting to do the Jews a favor, proposed a trial in Jerusalem, Paul used his right as a Roman citizen: “I appeal to Caesar.” He did this not because it gave him an advantage in court, but because, following the Lord’s promise, “you must testify also in Rome,” he judged that the time to go to Rome had come. God’s promise was certain, yet it was fulfilled through Paul’s wise choices and decisions. Sometimes God allows a slow, roundabout road instead of the fast and easy one, but even on that road His will does not stop. Paul did not preach to governors in spite of being a prisoner, but because he was one. There is no ideal environment for living out our faith. A person with a clear calling from the Lord lives a life that is simple, yet full.

“Take courage, for as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome.”
Acts 23:11
4Observation Questions
  1. What was the hidden motive behind the Jewish leaders’ request to bring Paul to Jerusalem? Even after two years, what was still missing from their accusations? Acts 25:3, 7–8
  2. Why did Festus offer Paul a trial in Jerusalem? How is Paul’s “I appeal to Caesar” connected to the Lord’s promise in 23:11? Acts 25:9–12; 23:11
  3. How does Festus describe Paul to King Agrippa? What does his report reveal about Paul? Acts 25:25–27
5Life Application
  1. What are your current occupations (student, employee, parent, church role, and so on)? Through them, what calling has the Lord entrusted to you?
    Are you simply holding a job, or living out a calling?
  2. After years of faith, is there an area (prayer, Scripture, worship) where you still feel clumsy? If the issue is love rather than ability, what is one thing you can do this week to rekindle your first love for the Lord?
  3. Is there an act of obedience you keep postponing “until things get better” or “until I have more time”? What would change if your present circumstances, and our church’s place, where there is much to do and little to show for it, became the very place of your calling?
6Praying Together
  • That, confessing we have lacked love more than ability, our first love for the Lord would be renewed and our hearts would beat for Him again
  • That whatever our occupation at school, at work, or at home, we would never forget our calling to live as the Lord’s people
  • That on slow, winding roads and in seasons of waiting, we would trust the God who walks with us, and choose and decide with wisdom
  • That the young people who pass through our church would be built up as disciples of Christ and sent out to churches in Korea and around the world
7Closing Prayer

Loving Father God, we have seen that though Paul was held as an unsentenced prisoner, the mission You gave him was never imprisoned, not for a single day. But Lord, we are so often bound by our occupations. Chasing better positions and more comfortable circumstances, we have forgotten the calling You entrusted to us. We say we are waiting for a better time to live out our faith, yet in truth we have been working to build a life we could live without You. Forgive us. We confess that what we lacked was not ability, but love. Like the man who found treasure hidden in a field, let us meet You anew as the One worth giving everything for, so that our lives may become simple, yet never empty, but full. When You lead us on a longer road instead of a faster one, help us trust that You walk that road with us. When we stumble or must wait, help us believe that Your will never stops. God, You planted our church in Champaign to serve the future of the Korean church. Though there is much to do and little seems to remain, let it be our joy to raise up the young people who pass through here as disciples of Christ and to send them out. Help us ask not “Can I?” but “Should I?” and press on toward the goal. We pray in the name of Jesus Christ, who calls us and sends us.

Amen.
Leader’s Notes

The Surrounding Context (24:27–26:32)

In last week’s passage, Governor Felix postponed his verdict and kept Paul in Caesarea for two years; when Porcius Festus succeeded him, Felix left Paul in prison to do the Jews a favor (24:27). Today’s passage takes place just after Festus arrived. Next, King Agrippa and Bernice visit Festus (25:13–22), and Festus brings Paul before them in hope of finding charges to send to the emperor (25:23–27). After Paul’s defense in chapter 26, Agrippa says, “This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar” (26:32). Paul eventually reaches Rome and spends two years in his own rented quarters, proclaiming the kingdom of God “with all boldness” (28:30–31).

From Felix to Festus

The sermon described Felix as recalled to Rome because of his cruelty and greed. According to the Jewish historian Josephus, when Jewish and Greek residents of Caesarea clashed, Felix crushed the Jews harshly with troops, and he was later accused before the emperor. His successor Festus (governor c. AD 59–62) is remembered as relatively fair, but he died in office. Going up to Jerusalem only three days after arriving (25:1) suggests he wanted to quickly settle this restless region.

The Appeal to Caesar (vv. 11–12)

A Roman citizen could ask to be tried by the emperor rather than by a provincial governor. The sermon calls this right provocatio; in the imperial period it was also known as appellatio. Once an appeal was accepted, the governor had to send the case to Rome. The “council” in verse 12 refers to the governor’s legal advisers. The emperor at the time was Nero (reigned AD 54–68). His persecution of Christians came after the Great Fire of Rome in AD 64, so when Paul appealed, Nero was not yet the notorious emperor he later became.

Occupation, Calling, and the Goal

The sermon title comes from Philippians 3:13–14: “But one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.” Paul is thought to have written Philippians while imprisoned in Rome. The word “call” (klēsis) here connects with the sermon’s theme of calling.

Facilitation Tips

  • Suggested time: Opening 10 min · Reading & Summary 15 min · Observation 15 min · Application 20 min · Prayer 15 min
  • Keep the sermon’s balance in view. It does not say we should simply accept our circumstances; Paul himself wisely used his Roman citizenship and the legal system. The point is not to make a better environment the goal of life, but to live out our calling right where we are.
  • In Application 2, keep the conversation from turning into self-condemnation (“I just don’t love God”). The sermon is an invitation to admit our lack, grieve over it, and begin our relationship with the Lord anew.
  • In Application 3, talking about the church’s challenges (much to do, people constantly coming and going) can slide into complaining. Bring the focus back to our church’s mission to serve “the future of the Korean church.” If someone is worn out from long years of service, acknowledge and encourage them first.
